What AARO is

The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) is a real, standing office inside the U.S. Department of Defense. It was formally established on July 20, 2022, under direction of the Deputy Secretary of Defense, pursuant to Section 1683 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022 — now codified as 50 U.S.C. §3373. That means AARO isn't a task force or a temporary initiative; it exists because Congress passed a law requiring it to.

It's the direct successor to two earlier, narrower efforts: the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF), stood up by the Navy in 2020, and the Airborne Object Identification and Management Group formed in late 2021. AARO consolidated those into a single, permanent, cross-domain office — the "all-domain" in its name reflects that it covers reports from air, sea, space, and land, not just aviation.

Its founding director was Dr. Sean Kirkpatrick (2022–2024); Dr. Jon Kosloski, previously a senior NSA researcher, has led the office since August 2024. AARO is overseen by an executive council chaired by the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security.

What AARO does

AARO's statutory mission is to minimize technical and intelligence surprise by synchronizing the detection, identification, attribution, and, if necessary, mitigation of unidentified anomalous phenomena near national security areas — military bases, training ranges, and other sensitive sites. In practice, that breaks down into a few concrete functions:

  • Intake and investigation. AARO receives and investigates UAP reports from military personnel, government agencies, and (through a dedicated reporting mechanism) contractors and former officials.
  • Case resolution. For select, high-profile cases, AARO publishes individual "case resolution reports" explaining what its analysis concluded — several of which (Aguadilla, Al Taqaddum, Mount Etna, and others) are cited as sources on this site.
  • Reporting to Congress. By law, AARO must deliver periodic reports summarizing UAP activity, including an annual Consolidated Annual Report on UAP and, in 2024, a multi-part Historical Record Report reviewing decades of prior U.S. government UAP-related activity.
  • Declassification. Since 2023, AARO has run a public website releasing declassified imagery, video, and documents. In 2026, this expanded into PURSUE (Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters), a rolling declassification effort ordered by the White House.
  • Congressional testimony. AARO's leadership and other witnesses have testified in multiple open congressional hearings since 2023, some of which are cited as sources in this atlas.

What "validity" means here

It's worth being precise about what is and isn't being claimed by calling AARO's reports "official." Two separate things are both true at once:

AARO itself, and its reports, are genuinely real. It is a legally-mandated federal office operating under congressional oversight, not a fringe group or a marketing exercise. Its annual reports, historical review, and individual case-resolution reports are real government documents, and the incidents catalogued in this atlas are drawn from those documents or from sworn congressional testimony — not from anonymous internet claims.

That does not mean every case is confirmed, or that anything anomalous has been verified. AARO has stated repeatedly, including in its Historical Record Report, that it has found no verified evidence that any UAP report represents extraterrestrial technology or activity. Of the cases AARO has been able to resolve, the large majority turn out to be conventional: weather balloons, drones, aircraft, satellites, birds, or sensor artifacts. A substantial share of cases remain formally unresolved — and under AARO's own definition, that specifically means there wasn't enough data to reach any conclusion, not that something inexplicable was confirmed.

This atlas mirrors that same distinction directly in its status labels: Unresolved (reviewed, no explanation reached), Explained (AARO or another government body reached a conventional explanation), and Uncorroborated (insufficient evidence to assess either way). None of the three should be read as confirmation of anything anomalous — they describe the state of the government's own investigation, not a verdict on what was actually seen.

Where this site's data comes from

The 71 cases in this atlas were compiled from AARO's Historical Record Report Volume I, AARO's Consolidated Annual Reports to Congress for fiscal years 2022–2025, AARO's individual case-resolution reports, and testimony from public congressional UAP hearings, cross-checked against corroborating news coverage. Each case entry lists its specific source. This site is an independent, unofficial compilation for personal reference — it is not produced, reviewed, or endorsed by AARO or the Department of Defense.
